The Complete Multilingual Website Guide

Serving customers in multiple languages is a massive competitive advantage. But doing it wrong - auto-translation, broken layouts, missing hreflang - can hurt you more than help.

June 2026 · 9 min read · WebMaxPro Blog

Why Multilingual Websites Matter More Than Ever

Less than 26% of the world's internet users speak English as their first language. Yet the vast majority of business websites are English-only - which means an enormous share of potential customers are being served in a language that isn't their own. Studies consistently show that people are significantly more likely to buy when they can interact with a website in their native language. In multilingual markets like Belgium (Dutch, French, German), Switzerland, Canada, and the UAE, a multilingual website isn't a luxury - it's a baseline expectation for any serious business.

Translation vs. Localisation: Understanding the Difference

Many businesses make the mistake of thinking translation and localisation are the same thing. They're not, and the difference matters enormously for your website's effectiveness.

Translation is the process of converting text from one language to another. It's necessary, but insufficient. Literal translations often sound unnatural, use the wrong register (formal vs. informal), or miss cultural nuances that make the difference between connecting with an audience and alienating them.

Localisation goes further: it adapts your content for the cultural context of each target market. This includes using locally appropriate idioms and expressions, adjusting pricing to local currencies and formats, referencing local laws or standards where relevant (e.g., GDPR for European audiences), using date and time formats that match the local convention, and ensuring images and examples feel culturally relevant. A website localised for the Belgian Dutch market will feel subtly different from one localised for the Netherlands - and your Dutch-speaking Belgian audience will notice and appreciate that care.

The Problem with Auto-Translation

Google Translate and AI translation tools have improved dramatically, but they remain unreliable for professional business communications. Auto-translated websites produce text that native speakers immediately recognise as machine-generated - awkward phrasing, wrong verb forms, inconsistent terminology, and occasional outright errors that undermine credibility. For a business investing in its professional image, auto-translation is counterproductive.

More importantly, auto-translated content cannot be indexed properly by search engines for multilingual SEO. If you want to rank in French search results, you need real French content on real French-language URLs - not JavaScript-rendered translation overlays that search engine crawlers struggle to process correctly.

URL Structure for Multilingual SEO

How you structure your multilingual website's URLs has a significant impact on SEO performance. There are three main approaches, each with different trade-offs:

  • Subdirectories: example.com/fr/ for French, example.com/nl/ for Dutch. This is generally the recommended approach for most businesses - it consolidates domain authority and is easiest to manage.
  • Subdomains: fr.example.com, nl.example.com. Technically cleaner separation but splits your domain authority and is more complex to set up correctly.
  • Country code top-level domains (ccTLDs): example.fr, example.nl. The strongest local SEO signal but requires registering and maintaining multiple domains.

For most small to medium businesses, the subdirectory approach (example.com/fr/) offers the best balance of SEO benefit, technical simplicity, and cost. Whichever you choose, be consistent and never mix approaches on the same site.

Implementing Hreflang Tags Correctly

Hreflang is an HTML attribute that tells search engines which language and regional version of a page exists for a given URL. Without hreflang, Google may show the wrong language version to users in different countries, or may see your translated pages as duplicate content rather than intentional language variants.

A correct hreflang implementation requires: a hreflang tag for every language version on every page, a self-referencing hreflang tag on each page, an x-default tag pointing to your fallback version, and absolute (not relative) URLs. Hreflang errors are extremely common and often go undetected for months, silently undermining international SEO performance. Google Search Console's International Targeting report will flag implementation errors.

Right-to-Left (RTL) Design for Arabic, Hebrew & Other RTL Languages

Arabic, Hebrew, Persian, and Urdu are written right-to-left - which means a website designed for these languages requires a fundamentally different layout approach, not just text translation. In a proper RTL implementation, the entire page layout mirrors: navigation moves to the right, text aligns right, icons and arrows reverse direction, and the reading flow goes from right to left.

This cannot be achieved by simply adding dir="rtl" to the body tag. Typography choices matter enormously - the default Latin fonts used in most websites render Arabic text poorly. You need Arabic-optimised fonts (like Noto Naskh Arabic, Cairo, or Tajawal) that render correctly at all sizes. Language Switching: UX Best Practices

How users switch between languages on your site matters more than most designers realise. Best practices include: placing the language selector in a consistent, prominent location (typically the top-right of navigation), using the language name in the target language (not flags - flags represent countries, not languages, and create problems for languages spoken across multiple countries), preserving the user's current page when switching (don't redirect to the homepage), and remembering the user's language preference across sessions with a cookie.

Avoid using flags alone as language selectors. A Belgian visitor who speaks French shouldn't have to choose between a Belgian and a French flag - use "Français" and "Nederlands" as text labels instead.
