Arabic Website Design Guide 2026

The Arab world has 400 million Arabic speakers and one of the fastest-growing digital economies on earth. Building a proper Arabic website requires much more than translation - here's what it really takes.

June 2026 · 9 min read · WebMaxPro Blog

The Scale of the Arabic Web Opportunity

Arabic is the fifth most spoken language in the world, with over 400 million native speakers across 22 countries. The Arab world's internet penetration is growing rapidly - the UAE and Saudi Arabia both have penetration rates above 99%, while Egypt, Jordan, and Morocco are experiencing some of the fastest mobile internet growth in the world. E-commerce in the Arab world is projected to exceed $50 billion USD by 2027. Yet the majority of businesses targeting Arab consumers still have inadequate Arabic web presences - often relying on poor translations, misaligned layouts, or no Arabic at all. The opportunity is enormous for businesses willing to invest in proper Arabic web design.

Understanding RTL: Right-to-Left Is Not Just Flipped Text

The most fundamental aspect of Arabic web design is the right-to-left (RTL) reading direction. Arabic script is read from right to left, which means the entire layout logic of a website needs to mirror. This isn't just a CSS text-align change - it's a complete rethinking of spatial relationships on the page.

In a proper RTL Arabic website:

  • Navigation menus appear on the right side and expand leftward
  • The logo typically sits on the right of the header
  • Text aligns right by default
  • Progress indicators, breadcrumbs, and pagination run right to left
  • Directional icons (arrows, chevrons) point in the opposite direction
  • Form fields align right, with labels to the right of inputs
  • Sidebars that appear on the left in LTR versions move to the right in RTL
  • Slide carousels navigate in the opposite direction

Many developers attempt RTL by simply adding dir="rtl" to the body tag, but this alone is insufficient. Proper RTL requires auditing every layout component individually and ensuring the spatial logic makes sense for right-to-left reading.

Arabic Typography: Choosing the Right Fonts

Arabic typography is a specialised craft. Arabic script is cursive - letters connect to each other differently depending on their position in a word - and different fonts handle this with wildly varying quality. Using a poor-quality Arabic font on a professional website immediately signals inexperience and undermines credibility.

Well-regarded Arabic web fonts in 2026 include:

  • Cairo: A versatile, modern Arabic font with excellent legibility at screen sizes. Available on Google Fonts.
  • Tajawal: Clean and minimal, pairs well with Latin fonts for bilingual sites.
  • Noto Naskh Arabic: Google's comprehensive Arabic font designed for maximum legibility across sizes.
  • Almarai: A contemporary typeface designed specifically for digital use in Arab markets.
  • IBM Plex Arabic: IBM's open-source Arabic typeface, excellent for professional and technical content.

Arabic text also requires larger base font sizes than Latin text for equivalent legibility - typically 18–20px body text rather than the 15–16px common in English sites. Line height also needs to be more generous, around 1.8–2.0, to accommodate the ascenders and descenders of Arabic script.

Arabic SEO: Ranking on Google in Arabic

Arabic SEO has some unique characteristics that require specific approaches. Arabic keywords vary significantly by country and dialect - the word used for "lawyer" in Egyptian Arabic differs from Gulf Arabic. For businesses targeting a specific country, keyword research must be done for that country's variant of Arabic, not Modern Standard Arabic alone.

Key Arabic SEO considerations:

  • Use the correct Arabic locale in your hreflang tags (ar-SA for Saudi Arabia, ar-AE for UAE, ar-EG for Egypt, etc.)
  • Research keywords using Google Keyword Planner with the target country selected and the language set to Arabic
  • Ensure your meta titles and descriptions are written in Arabic for Arabic-language pages - don't leave them in English
  • Arabic users frequently search using both Arabic script and romanised Arabic (known as Arabizi) - consider both in your keyword strategy
  • Build backlinks from Arabic-language websites in your target market for strong regional authority signals

Bilingual Arabic/English Websites: Architecture Decisions

Most businesses targeting Arab markets also need an English version - for international clients, expat communities, and B2B contexts where English is the business language. The technical approach to bilingual Arabic/English sites requires careful planning.

The recommended URL structure is subdirectories: yoursite.com/ar/ for Arabic content and yoursite.com/en/ for English. Each directory should have its own independently optimised content - not just translated pages with the same content, but content adapted to the different audiences. The Arabic homepage might emphasise trust signals that matter to Arab clients (years in market, local certifications, Arabic testimonials), while the English version might foreground international credentials and case studies.

The language switcher should be visible and accessible from every page, using text labels ("العربية" and "English") rather than flags. The switch should keep the user on the equivalent page in the other language, not redirect to the homepage.

Cultural Considerations in Arabic Web Design

Beyond the technical requirements, successful Arabic websites account for cultural expectations that differ from Western web design norms. Arab consumers place particular value on trust signals - detailed "About" pages with real team photos and credentials, clear physical address and phone number visibility, and testimonials from recognisable clients are more important than in many Western markets.

Visual aesthetics in Arab markets trend toward richer colour palettes and more decorative elements than the minimalism popular in European design. Luxury and premium positioning are communicated through gold accents, calligraphic elements, and generous use of white space rather than stark minimalism. However, this is market and sector-dependent - a tech startup in Dubai has very different design expectations from a traditional business in Cairo.
